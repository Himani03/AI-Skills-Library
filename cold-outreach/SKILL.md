# Cold outreach

Use this for any message Himani sends to a person about a job: cold emails to hiring managers or founders, LinkedIn connection notes and DMs, InMail, replies to recruiters, conference follow-ups, referral asks, and follow-ups after silence.

## Voice (every time, without being asked)

- Sounds like her talking: casual, warm, conversational. Contractions, plain words, a mix of short and medium sentences.
- No em dashes. No corporate or AI-sounding phrasing. Banned: "I hope this email finds you well", "I am writing to express my interest", "seamless", "leverage", "passionate", "excited to leverage", and anything that reads like the job description or the company's tagline repeated back.
- Read it as if she were saying it out loud to someone she respects. If it sounds like a template, rewrite it.

## Accuracy

- Only claim things she has actually done. Use her real projects and work history, and check her saved notes and earlier conversations for the details.
- Don't invent shared connections, conversations, or details about the other person or company. If you don't know what she and the person talked about, ask her.
- Every fact about the company must come from the research below, not from memory or guesswork.

## Step 1: Research the company (required, before drafting)

Do this every time, even for a quick reply, unless she has already given you the company's details in this conversation.

1. Search the web for the company and, if there is one, the person or team. Look at what they actually build, who it's for, the engineering or product problem they're working on, recent launches or news, their blog or docs, and the specific role or team.
2. Fetch one or two of the best pages (product page, engineering blog, recent announcement) instead of relying on search snippets.
3. Pick one or two specific things that stand out. Specific means a product decision, a technical problem, a recent launch, or a way they approach something. Not their mission statement or tagline.
4. If the research turns up nothing specific, say so and ask her what draws her to them. Don't make up a reason.

## Step 2: Write the "why I'm interested" (two sentences)

- Two sentences, in her voice, personalized to this company.
- Sentence one names the specific thing from the research and why it caught her attention. Sentence two connects it to something real she has built or done, so it's clear why she cares and why it fits.
- Plain and genuine. No flattery, no "I love your mission", no generic praise that could be pasted into any other email.
- If the research and her background don't connect honestly, don't force it. Tell her and offer a different angle.

## Step 3: Identify the person and pick a proof point

1. Who it's going to and how she knows them: met in person (conference, job fair), 1st-degree LinkedIn connection, total cold, recruiter who reached out, or a follow-up after silence.
2. One proof point from her work that fits what they do, with a repo link if it helps. One is enough, and it can be the same project used in the "why I'm interested" sentence.

## Structure

- **Subject line** (email): short and specific, not salesy.
- **Opener:** one line, either the conference moment or the connection to them.
- **Why I'm interested:** the two personalized sentences from step 2.
- **Why she's relevant:** one or two sentences with the proof point, only if it isn't already covered above.
- **The ask:** small and easy to say yes to (a quick chat, being considered for a role, or pointing her to the right person). One ask only.
- **Sign-off:** simple and friendly.

## Length by type

- Cold email: about 100 to 150 words including the two interest sentences.
- LinkedIn connection note: under 200 characters (free accounts cap lower than premium). Squeeze the interest into one short clause.
- LinkedIn DM or InMail: about 70 to 110 words.
- Recruiter reply: short and direct. Confirm interest, answer what they asked, mention availability or logistics. Add one line on why the company interests her only if it helps.
- Conference follow-up: reference something specific from the conversation, add the interest sentences, and ask for time.
- Follow-up after silence: one short nudge with a new angle or new proof point, not a repeat of the first message. At most two, spaced about a week apart.

## Delivery

- Give one strong draft by default. Offer a couple of subject line options for emails.
- After the draft, add one line saying what the interest sentences were based on, with the source link, so she can check it.
- Mention which resume version to attach if it matters.
- If a Gmail connector is available and she asks, save it as a draft. Never send without her explicitly saying to.
- Before handing over, check: no em dashes, no banned phrases, every claim is true and sourced, the interest sentences could only be about this company, and there is exactly one ask.
